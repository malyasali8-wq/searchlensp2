Why Is My Website Not Showing Up on Google? 7 Fixes With Real Search Console Data

By Muhammad Ilyas, SEOGlint

Blog · Published November 28, 2026 · Original website observations collected on November 28, 2026

Is Google not indexing your pages, or is it indexing them without ranking them? Here is how to tell, with real SEOGlint Search Console data.

You launched your website, submitted your sitemap, optimized your pages, and waited for Google to discover your content. But when you search for your products, services, or target keywords, your website is nowhere to be found.

Does that mean Google has not indexed your website?

Not necessarily.

At SEOGlint, we encountered an interesting example while reviewing our own website's search performance. Our website scanner reported an optimization score of 98 out of 100, and Google Search Console reported 30 indexed pages with zero non-indexed pages.

Yet our Search Performance report showed only six clicks from 940 impressions, with an average position of 74.7.

This illustrates why website owners must distinguish indexing problems from ranking problems before attempting to fix them.

In this guide, you will learn how to diagnose both situations using practical troubleshooting steps, real Search Console observations, and official Google documentation.

Quick Answer: Why Is My Website Not Showing Up on Google?

Your website may not appear on Google because its pages have not been discovered, crawled, or indexed. Alternatively, the pages may already be indexed but rank poorly for the keywords you are searching.

Common issues include:

  • Recently published pages that Google has not processed.
  • Unintentional robots.txt restrictions.
  • Noindex directives.
  • Server or page accessibility problems.
  • Duplicate URLs and canonical conflicts.
  • Weak internal links.
  • Content that does not sufficiently satisfy the searcher's needs.
  • Competitive search results.

Start by inspecting the exact page URL in Google Search Console. The indexing result determines which troubleshooting steps you should follow.

Crawling, Indexing, and Ranking: Understand the Difference

Google Search processes websites through different stages.

Stage What happens
Discovery Google becomes aware of a URL through links, sitemaps, or other sources.
Crawling Google attempts to retrieve and process the page.
Indexing Google analyzes the page and may include it in its search index.
Ranking and serving Google selects and orders relevant results for a user's search.

A website can pass the technical checks necessary for indexing without achieving strong rankings.

Similarly, a URL can be discovered without being crawled, and a crawled page may remain outside Google's index.

Understanding these distinctions prevents unnecessary changes and helps you focus on the actual problem.

For more information, see Google's guide to how Search works.

Fix 1: Check Whether Your Website Is Indexed

Before changing your website, determine whether the affected page is actually in Google's index.

Method A: Perform a quick Google search

Search Google using the following format:

site:yourdomain.com

For a specific page, try:

site:yourdomain.com/your-page

This can provide a quick indication of whether Google returns pages from your domain.

However, the site search operator is not a complete or definitive Google index checker. Missing results do not necessarily prove that a URL is unindexed.

Method B: Use Google Search Console

For a website you own or manage:

  1. Open Google Search Console.
  2. Select your verified website property.
  3. Enter the exact page URL into the URL Inspection tool.
  4. Review the indexing verdict.
  5. Expand the Page indexing section for additional details.

If Google reports that the URL is indexed, investigate its search visibility rather than immediately treating it as an indexing failure.

If the URL is not indexed, examine the reported reason and follow the relevant troubleshooting steps.

Fix 2: Diagnose Google Indexing Issues in Search Console

Open the Page Indexing report under the Indexing section of Search Console.

This report helps identify groups of URLs that Google has indexed or excluded.

Use the following diagnostic table to determine your next action.

Search Console status What to investigate
Page is indexed Search performance and ranking
Discovered – currently not indexed Discovery, crawling, and server accessibility
Crawled – currently not indexed Rendered content, duplication, and indexing selection
Blocked by robots.txt Unintended crawl restrictions
Excluded by noindex tag Robots meta tags and HTTP headers
Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt Whether the URL should be accessible or excluded
Duplicate or alternate page Canonical signals and duplicate URLs
Not found (404) Whether the URL should exist
Server error (5xx) Hosting, application, or server reliability

Not every excluded page represents a problem. Redirects, duplicate URLs, intentionally private pages, and removed content may be excluded as expected.

Google Indexing Troubleshooter

How this works: this is a status-based educational tool. You choose the status you see in Google Search Console, and it shows general next steps from the guidance in this article and Google's documentation. It does not connect to Search Console, does not scan your website and cannot see Google's index.

Select a status to see what to check next.

Crawled – Currently Not Indexed

This status indicates that Google retrieved the page but has not included it in its index at the time of reporting.

Investigate:

  • Whether the main content renders correctly.
  • Whether other URLs contain substantially similar information.
  • Whether the page has conflicting canonical signals.
  • Whether the content provides a distinct reason for users to visit it.

Do not assume that this status proves poor content quality. Search Console does not necessarily identify the specific reason Google declined to index an individual page.

Discovered – Currently Not Indexed

Google knows about the URL, but it has not yet been crawled.

Check whether the page:

  • Appears in a valid sitemap.
  • Has crawlable internal links from relevant pages.
  • Is served by a reliable website.
  • Is unnecessarily duplicated across different URL variations.

For recently published pages, allow Google time to process the URL. Repeatedly requesting indexing is not a substitute for resolving discovery or accessibility issues.

See Google's official Page Indexing report documentation for additional status explanations.

Fix 3: Check Robots.txt and Noindex Directives

Robots.txt and noindex directives serve different purposes.

Robots.txt controls crawler access, while noindex instructs supported search engines not to include a resource in search results when they can retrieve that directive.

Inspect your robots.txt file

Visit:

https://yourdomain.com/robots.txt

An example of a rule that blocks compliant crawlers from the entire website is:

Example robots.txt rule
User-agent: *
Disallow: /

If this rule was accidentally carried over from a development or staging website, it could prevent Google from crawling pages intended for public search visibility.

Do not remove intentional restrictions without understanding their purpose.

You can use the SEOGlint Robots.txt Generator to prepare rules, but always review them before deployment.

Check for noindex

Inspect the page's HTML source and HTTP response headers.

A robots meta tag containing noindex or an HTTP X-Robots-Tag header containing noindex may prevent indexing.

Remove unintended noindex directives from pages that should appear in search results.

What does "Indexed, though blocked by robots.txt" mean?

Google may discover and index a URL through links even when robots.txt prevents it from reading the page content.

If you want that page indexed normally, allow appropriate crawl access.

If you want to exclude a public page from Google, use a suitable indexing exclusion method and allow Google to retrieve the directive. Blocking crawling alone is not a reliable removal method.

For confidential content, use authentication rather than relying on robots.txt or noindex.

Reference: Google's noindex documentation.

Fix 4: Check Website Accessibility and Canonical URLs

Google needs to retrieve the public content you want it to process.

A website may appear normal in your browser while encountering difficulties during automated crawling.

Check for:

  • Unexpected HTTP errors.
  • Redirect loops or chains.
  • Authentication requirements.
  • Firewall restrictions.
  • Missing content during rendering.
  • Incorrect canonical tags.
  • Broken internal links.

Use the Test Live URL feature

Inspect the affected page in Search Console and select Test Live URL.

This helps you investigate current accessibility and certain technical eligibility checks.

A successful live test does not mean the page has been indexed. It also does not guarantee that Google will select the URL for indexing.

Verify canonical selection

A canonical URL identifies the preferred version among duplicate or substantially similar pages.

For example, a website might have multiple URL variations:

  • https://example.com/page
  • https://www.example.com/page
  • https://www.example.com/page?ref=campaign

When these represent the same content, consistent redirects, canonical annotations, internal links, and sitemap entries can help Google understand the preferred URL.

Compare the user-declared canonical with the Google-selected canonical in Search Console.

If they differ, investigate why Google may prefer another URL before making changes.

Reference: Google's canonical URL guidelines.

Fix 5: Submit Your Website to Google Using an XML Sitemap

An XML sitemap provides search engines with a list of URLs you want them to discover.

It is particularly useful for newly launched websites and websites with content that is difficult to discover through links alone.

Practical sitemap generation test

We tested the SEOGlint XML Sitemap Generator using three URLs:

  • https://www.seoglint.com/
  • https://www.seoglint.com/schema-generator.html
  • https://www.seoglint.com/schema-markup-validator.html

The generator displayed an XML sitemap containing three corresponding URL entries, using UTF-8 encoding.

The demonstration also included a weekly change frequency, priority of 0.8, and a last-modified date of November 28, 2026.

There is an important limitation here: the generator's automatic date option was enabled. The generated dates do not establish that each page was significantly modified on that day.

For production sitemaps, use accurate page modification dates or omit the optional lastmod field when the date is unknown.

Google ignores the priority and change frequency fields, so increasing these values will not improve search rankings.

A minimal XML sitemap example

Example sitemap
<?xml version="1.0" encoding="UTF-8"?>
<urlset xmlns="http://www.sitemaps.org/schemas/sitemap/0.9">
  <url>
    <loc>https://www.seoglint.com/</loc>
  </url>
  <url>
    <loc>https://www.seoglint.com/schema-generator.html</loc>
  </url>
  <url>
    <loc>https://www.seoglint.com/schema-markup-validator.html</loc>
  </url>
</urlset>

This is an illustrative three-URL sitemap, not a replacement for SEOGlint's complete production sitemap.

How to submit a sitemap

  1. Create an XML sitemap containing your intended canonical URLs.
  2. Publish the file at an accessible URL, commonly /sitemap.xml.
  3. Open Google Search Console.
  4. Select Sitemaps under the Indexing section.
  5. Enter the sitemap location and submit it.
  6. Monitor the processing status and investigate any reported errors.

You can also use the SEOGlint Sitemap Checker when investigating sitemap-related problems.

Fix 6: Request Indexing After Resolving Actual Problems

If you have published an important new page or corrected an indexing issue, you can request that Google revisit the URL.

Follow these steps:

  1. Open URL Inspection in Google Search Console.
  2. Enter the exact canonical URL.
  3. Review the existing result.
  4. Run a live test when investigating technical changes.
  5. Resolve unintended crawl or indexing restrictions.
  6. Select Request Indexing when appropriate.
  7. Monitor the result over time.

Google does not guarantee a specific indexing deadline. Crawling may take days or weeks, and submitting the same URL repeatedly does not accelerate processing.

For websites containing many new URLs, maintain an accurate sitemap and strong internal navigation instead of relying exclusively on individual indexing requests.

See Google's official instructions for requesting a recrawl.

Fix 7: Investigate Rankings When Your Website Is Already Indexed

This is where many website owners misdiagnose the problem.

If your URL is indexed but does not appear for your target keywords, the issue may be search relevance or competitiveness rather than indexing.

Open the Search Performance report in Google Search Console.

Examine:

Impressions: Whether your pages are appearing in search results.

Clicks: Whether users are selecting those results.

Average CTR: How frequently impressions result in clicks.

Average position: The average reported search-result position across the relevant impressions.

Review these metrics by page, query, country, and device rather than relying only on site-wide averages.

If your page has impressions but few clicks

Investigate its ranking position, search intent, page title, and how the result appears in the search results.

A low CTR on a poorly positioned page does not necessarily indicate an ineffective title. Position and the types of searches generating impressions can significantly influence click behavior.

If your page has few or no impressions

Check whether:

  • The page addresses a topic people search for.
  • The content matches the intended search query.
  • Relevant internal pages link to it.
  • The page offers information that distinguishes it from existing results.
  • The target queries are highly competitive.
  • The selected reporting period is sufficiently informative.

Instead of making unnecessary indexing requests, focus on improving the page's usefulness and relevance.

What this case study teaches us

A high website audit score and a positive indexing report do not automatically translate into high search rankings.

Our observations demonstrate that a website may simultaneously have:

  • Successfully indexed pages.
  • A successfully processed sitemap.
  • Strong scores under an automated audit tool's criteria.
  • Search impressions.
  • Relatively low organic clicks.

These measurements describe different aspects of website performance.

Technical SEO tools can help identify potential improvements, but they cannot guarantee how Google will rank content.

For website owners in a similar situation, the next useful step is to investigate page-level search performance and improve content based on actual user needs.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I check if my website is indexed by Google?

Use Google Search Console's URL Inspection tool to check a specific URL on your verified website. A site search on Google can provide a quick indication, but it should not be treated as a comprehensive indexing test.

Why is my website indexed but not showing up on Google?

The page may rank too low for the query you are searching, or it may not be selected as a relevant result for that query. Review impressions, position, search intent, and page-level performance in Search Console.

How long does Google take to index a new website?

There is no universal indexing deadline. Crawling may take days or weeks, and some URLs may never be selected for indexing. Reliable accessibility, internal links, and an accurate sitemap can help discovery.

Can I force Google to index my website?

No. You can request indexing and improve the conditions that support discovery and crawling, but Google ultimately determines which URLs enter its index.

Does submitting a sitemap improve Google rankings?

A sitemap helps communicate URL information to search engines. Submission does not directly guarantee improved rankings or inclusion in Google's index.

What is the best Google index checker?

For websites you own, the URL Inspection tool in Google Search Console provides direct information about individual URLs. Third-party tools may provide useful technical checks, but their capabilities and data limitations should be understood.

Does a 100% SEO score guarantee first-page rankings?

No. Automated SEO scores measure selected checks according to a tool's methodology. Google uses its own ranking systems and evaluates results in relation to individual searches.

Do Google indexing issues differ between countries?

The fundamental principles of crawling and indexing apply internationally. Search rankings and visibility, however, can vary according to language, location, search intent, competition, and other contextual factors.

Final Thoughts

If your website is not showing up on Google, do not immediately assume that indexing is the problem.

Start with evidence.

Inspect the affected URL, investigate the reported status, and fix the specific issue. If the page is already indexed, shift your attention toward search relevance, content usefulness, and actual performance.

SEOGlint's own observations show why this distinction matters: 30 reported indexed pages and a 98/100 internal audit score were accompanied by 940 impressions and just six search clicks.

The goal is not simply to get pages into Google's index. It is to create pages that deserve to be found and are useful when people visit them.
